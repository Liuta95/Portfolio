HealthTech · Multi-role SaaS · Web & Mobile

HealthConnect

A multi-role healthcare platform connecting patients, doctors and attorneys through one coordinated workflow, for people moving between medical care and legal support after a car accident.

Role
UX/UI Designer
Timeline
Mar 2024 – Jan 2025
Domain
HealthTech · Personal-injury care

Project Overview

HealthConnect is a multi-role healthcare SaaS platform built around a specific, high-stakes moment: someone injured in a car accident who needs to reach a doctor and a lawyer fast, without the process falling apart between the two.

Four roles work off the same case without seeing the same interface. The design challenge was building connected but role-specific workflows in a high-trust, data-heavy environment, where getting the information architecture wrong puts someone's care or case at risk.

I owned the product design across web and mobile: patient onboarding, appointment scheduling, video consultations, clinical documentation, CRM tools for the care team and the design system underneath them.

My role

Role & Contributions

Over about ten months I worked with the CEO, a product manager, backend and frontend engineers and QA. I designed one connected system rather than four separate apps, and stayed with the engineers through implementation.

  • Intake Form Restructuring: Took apart the long medical intake form with the CEO and a physician and rebuilt it as thematic steps with visible progress.
  • Role-based Flows: Gave each role its own entry point and information density: a guided flow for patients, a document-first queue for doctors, a filtered case view for attorneys and a full CRM table for the care team.
  • Clinical Documentation: Replaced free-text charting with a structured consultation report, where chief-complaint chips filter a coded diagnosis list.
  • Referral Chain: Kept patient → doctor → attorney visible on every visit record, so coordination didn't depend on someone remembering to loop the other side in.
  • Design System & Handoff: Built shared status pills, chip filters, data tables and document cards for all four roles, and delivered responsive high-fidelity interfaces and interactive prototypes.
Research

Discovery & Working Sessions

The heaviest patient-facing piece was the medical intake form: a long, dense questionnaire a patient had to finish before their first visit, often days after an accident. Before redesigning anything, I took it apart.

  • Form analysis
  • CEO + physician
  • Working sessions
Form analysis
Collected every question into Figma, then analysed how they were grouped, where the same information was asked twice and which sections demanded the most effort.
Working sessions
Reviewed the form with the CEO and a physician who knew these questionnaires from practice: the physician confirmed what was clinically necessary, the CEO what the business needed to keep.
Main risk
Completion was most likely to break down not on one question but on the length and medical density of the form as a whole.
Not done
Interviews with doctors or attorneys, competitive analysis and usability testing were not part of this project.
Decisions from the sessions
1

Thematic steps

Split the form into a few focused sections with visible progress instead of one long scroll.

2

Merge and remove

Merged overlapping questions and removed the ones that duplicated information already collected elsewhere.

3

Clinical core intact

Kept clinically required questions, changing their order and grouping, not their substance.

The hardest trade-off was the completeness doctors needed against what an injured patient could realistically finish. Beyond the form itself, I explored ways to support completion: reminders for unfinished profiles, help from care-team staff, and filling the form in together with the patient on a call.

Part 1 · System

Four roles, one case

Patient-facing journeys, doctor workflows, attorney access and internal CRM operations had to stay consistent with each other, not only polished on their own.

1

Patients

A calm, guided experience for registration, health information, booking and virtual consultations, not a clinical form dump.

2

Doctors

Fast access to cases, visit details, clinical statuses, referrals and whatever is still outstanding before or after an appointment.

3

Attorneys

Visibility into relevant case progress without exposure to clinical workflows that aren't theirs to see.

4

Care team

CRM tools to manage patient data, assignments, warnings, communications and workflow states at scale.

Part 2 · Web

The doctor's workflow

A doctor's day runs from a work list, through a structured visit form, to a record that is reviewed and signed rather than re-entered. The logo is a placeholder mark from the design file, not the client's branding.

Part 3 · Mobile

The patient's path to a first visit

After an accident, a patient has to share what happened, describe their symptoms and book a video visit, often while in pain. One long form became short steps, every step says what comes next, and help is a tab instead of a floating button. Built for iPhone (390 × 844) and checked against Apple's and Material's guidelines for touch targets, text size and contrast.

Part 4 · Mobile

The video visit

The production call handled the basics: participants, mute, camera, leave and a few error dialogs. The new flow covers the whole visit instead: checking the device before joining, knowing why the doctor is late, staying in the call when the connection or camera fails, and leaving with clear next steps. People in the call are illustrated stand-ins, not real patients.

Part 5 · Design system

One system, two platforms

Web and mobile share one set of tokens: colour, spacing, radius and states. What changes is scale. Clinicians work on large screens with a mouse, patients on a phone with one thumb, so each platform gets its own type scale and control sizes.

Shared rules
1

4 pt grid

Every padding, gap and corner radius is a multiple of 4, bound to spacing and radius tokens, so web and mobile line up.

2

Semantic colour

Components use roles such as text/secondary or warning/subtle, never raw hex, so one blue accent and its states change in one place.

3

Contrast and status

Text meets WCAG AA 4.5:1, including disabled labels. Status is always a word on a badge, with colour only as support.

4

Every state drawn

Default, hover, focus and disabled exist for each control. Focus is a 2 pt ring offset from the edge, so keyboard users can see where they are.

Web guidelines
1

Dense, but readable

Clinicians scan long queues, so body text is 13–14 px and buttons are 32–40 px for a mouse, with a 28 px page title.

2

Status leads the row

A row’s tint, badge and single action all follow the document status, so the next step is obvious without opening anything.

Mobile guidelines
1

Touch targets

Buttons and fields have a mobile size L of 48 pt, above Apple’s 44 pt and Material’s 48 dp minimums. Icon buttons get a 48 pt hit area.

2

Type for reading

A separate mobile scale on iOS Dynamic Type sizes: body 17 pt, footnote 13, captions no smaller than 11.

3

Platform patterns

iOS status bar, a 44 pt navigation bar with a back chevron, a 49 pt tab bar, switches for consent and bottom sheets with a grabber.

4

Checked twice

Screens were audited against Apple’s Human Interface Guidelines and Material Design for targets, text size, contrast and the 4 pt grid, then fixed.

System states

Illustrations for empty, waiting and error states

A small illustration set for the moments when there is nothing to act on, something is waiting on someone else, or something went wrong. Each one keeps the same soft accent circle and puts a single status mark on a document or device, so the state reads at a glance.

Results

No usability testing or patient-feedback round was run in this project, so there are no before-and-after numbers. Design decisions were validated with the CEO and the physician.

Intake form

Thematic steps

Restructured with visible progress and a way to save and come back. Overlaps merged, duplicates removed, clinical questions kept.

Roles

4 in one system

Patients, doctors, attorneys and the care team on one shared component foundation instead of one-off screens per surface.

Clinical documentation

Coded, not free text

A structured consultation report with chip-driven intake and suggested ICD-10 codes replaced free-text charting.

Next to measure

Completion per step

If the project continued, I'd measure where patients actually drop off in the form, rather than where I expect them to.

Designing four roles into one connected system taught me that consistency isn't about making every screen look the same. It's about keeping the same case legible from four different vantage points, without showing anyone more than their role needs to see.
